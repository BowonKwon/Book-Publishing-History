Chapter 2. Printing spreads across Europe (1450–1800)

Key point. Counting editions directly in three open union catalogs (ISTC, VD, STCN), the share of Latin books falls toward the late eighteenth century in both the German-speaking lands and the Netherlands. In the German-speaking lands it is 50.5 percent in 1650–1699 and 22.8 percent in 1750–1799. These are counts of books that survive and are cataloged today, not of everything printed at the time.

Counting what survives

Books printed between 1450 and 1800 have been recorded edition by edition in national union catalogs. This chapter uses three of them that can be downloaded and recounted.

Catalog Coverage Editions counted here Terms
ISTC (Incunabula Short Title Catalogue, British Library) Books printed with movable type in the 15th century 30,500 (87 undated excluded; 28,530 dated 1500 or earlier) CC BY-NC 4.0; counts only
VD16, VD17, VD18 (prints of the German-speaking lands) 1501–1800 669,442 (24 dated outside this range kept separately) CC0
STCN (Short-Title Catalogue, Netherlands) Before 1800 198,370 (19,389 dated only to a decade or century, and 2 after 1830, excluded) CC0

The counting rules were fixed before counting (see Method). In VD, single volumes of multivolume works, periodicals, and works bound into or contained in other works were left out; monographs and multivolume works as a whole were counted once each. That gives 108,200 editions in VD16, 296,708 in VD17, and 265,508 in VD18. These rules account for part of the difference from the project sizes given in the VD datasheet (about 106,000, 315,000 and 295,000); since the datasheet does not say exactly what those figures count, the difference cannot be fully broken down.

The VD line has vertical marks at 1601 and 1701, because the three projects follow different cataloging rules. The VD datasheet says that VD16 and VD17 examine all known copies of an edition to take print variants into account, while VD18 records one copy per edition. The annual averages of 3,724 editions in 1690–1700 and 2,655 in 1701–1710 are therefore hard to compare directly. VD18 is also still in progress.

The 1520s

In the German-speaking lands the annual average is 374 editions in 1501–1510 and 493 in 1511–1517. It rises to 1,193 in 1518–1525, then stands at 931 in 1526–1530. Dates alone do not say what produced this change. In the same decade, however, the share of editions whose first language is Latin is 31.6 percent, well below the 71.1 percent of the 1510s. It is also the decade in which Wittenberg first enters the five largest places of publication.

The share of Latin

In the ISTC, 79.7 percent of fifteenth-century books in the 1470s and 66.0 percent in the 1490s are in Latin. In VD, the share of editions whose first language is Latin is 46.4 percent in 1500–1549, 56.5 percent in 1600–1649, 50.5 percent in 1650–1699, 39.4 percent in 1700–1749, and 22.8 percent in 1750–1799. In the STCN, editions recorded only in Latin make up 28.5 percent in 1600–1649 and 13.5 percent in 1750–1799. STCN languages are an unordered list, so editions with two or more languages are grouped as "multiple."

Places of publication

Among VD editions with a recorded place, the five largest places account for 69.9 percent in the 1500s (Leipzig, Cologne, Strasbourg, Nuremberg, Augsburg). The share falls to 34.7 percent by the 1580s, stays between 31.7 and 37.2 percent through the seventeenth century, and between 27.0 and 34.1 percent in the eighteenth. The number of distinct places is 46 in the 1500s, 332 in the 1680s, and 528 in the 1770s. Editions without a recorded place, between 2.6 and 21.3 percent per decade, are left out of this calculation.

Set against the half-century estimates

The Buringh–van Zanden estimates seen in Chapter 1 are half-century totals for thirteen Western European regions. They rise from 12,587,000 in 1450–1500 to 641,166,000 in 1750–1800. These values seem to be editions multiplied by copies per edition rather than editions (Chapter 1), so they are not drawn on the same axis as the edition counts above.

England

The register of the Stationers' Company of London holds 10,453 entries for 1557–1640. The sister site From Privilege to Patent counts them year by year. Entry was not compulsory until 1637, so the number of entries is not the number of books published.
