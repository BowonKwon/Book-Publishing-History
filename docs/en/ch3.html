Chapter 3. Joseon woodblocks and the books that survive (16th–19th centuries)

Key point. No series links Joseon Korea's printing output year by year. What survives are lists of where printing woodblocks were kept at a given time, and catalogs of the old books that libraries hold today. The first give two snapshots, 989 sets in the late sixteenth century and 610 works in 1796; the second give a distribution of surviving holding records.

Two woodblock lists

Lists of where the carved blocks used to print books (chaekpan) were kept survive from two points in time.

The woodblock list in Gosa chwaryo (攷事撮要, "Selected essentials of past affairs") appears in editions printed in 1568, 1576 and 1585. According to the Encyclopedia of Korean Culture it lists 989 sets of blocks: 387 in Gyeongsang, 337 in Jeolla, 77 in Hwanghae, 67 in Pyeongan, 61 in Gangwon, 37 in Chungcheong, 19 in Hamgyeong and 2 in Gyeonggi. These provincial counts add up to 987, two fewer than the total.

Nupango (鏤板考, "A study of woodblocks") was completed in 1796. It is attributed to Seo Yu-gu; the original is lost, and manuscript copies are held at Kyujanggak and Korea University Library. It lists 610 works, including 66 composed or compiled by royal order: 47 classics, 74 histories, 119 philosophy and technical works, and 304 literary collections.

The two lists differ in unit (sets and works) and in scope, so a fall from 989 to 610 cannot be read from them. Kim Chi-woo (1999) counted 1,292 titles in the woodblock lists of early Joseon, or 1,014 after removing duplicates (secondary).

Old books that survive today

The Korean Old and Rare Collection Information System (KORCIS) of the National Library of Korea brings together the catalogs of old books held by institutions in Korea and abroad. Its open API can only be searched by term, so the results of twenty terms fixed in advance were collected and deduplicated by record number (see Method). That yields 272,115 records, a little over half of the roughly 510,000 reported on Korea's public data portal; the distribution here is a sample, not the whole.

The date field is free text. Some records give a year in the Western calendar, as in "1899 (光武3)"; others say "[刊寫年未詳]" (date of printing or copying unknown), "[朝鮮後期]" (late Joseon), or give only a sexagenary year. Reading all 272,115 records gives the following.

Leaving out those after 1910 leaves 207,618 records, of which 46.2 percent have a readable year. The chart divides those dated 1350–1899 into fifty-year bins (records before 1350, and the eleven years 1900–1910, are in the table). The largest is 1850–1899 with 31,389 records, followed by 1800–1849 with 14,310 and 1750–1799 with 14,047.

This chart does not show output. Only books that survive are counted, and a book held by several institutions is counted several times. Grouping records with the same title, edition type, date string and publisher gives 25,566 "edition-like groups" for 1850–1899, 19 percent fewer than the records. More than half of the records have no readable year, which also has to be kept in view.

Japan

New titles in Edo-period Japan were summarized as long-period annual averages in Peter Kornicki's work. In the per capita chart of Chapter 4, Japan's values before 1911 are those averages as taken over by Fink-Jensen (secondary). Statistics of the Meiji Home Ministry (titles published, 1884–1936) are said to be in the National Diet Library Digital Collections, but the original tables are not transcribed in this version.
